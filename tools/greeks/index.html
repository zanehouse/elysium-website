Units. This is where options calculators most often mislead, because the same greek gets quoted several different ways. Time is in years on an ACT/365 basis. Delta is per $1 of underlying. Gamma is the change in delta per $1. Vega is shown per one volatility point, meaning a move from 25% to 26%, which is the trading convention; the raw mathematical derivative is 100× larger. Theta is shown per calendar day, not per trading day and not per year, and runs negative for a long option. Rho is shown per 1%, or 100bp, of rate. Rate and dividend yield are entered as percentages and treated as continuously compounded.

The model. Black-Scholes-Merton with a continuous dividend yield, the standard European pricing model. It assumes constant volatility and lognormal returns, so it will not reproduce the volatility smile visible in real option quotes; that smile is the market disagreeing with this exact assumption. It also prices European exercise, so American options on dividend-paying underlyings can be worth more, and the gap is widest for in-the-money puts. The model is best used to understand sensitivities and relative behavior rather than as a source of fair value.

The implied-volatility solver inverts the price with a bracketed Newton method, so it converges for any quote inside the no-arbitrage bounds. When no solution exists, it reports that instead of returning a plausible-looking number: below intrinsic value, above the discounted underlying, or where the contract carries essentially no time value. That last case is real and worth understanding, because a deep in-the-money contract can be expensive and still encode nothing about volatility when all of its price is intrinsic. Volatility is carried entirely by time value.

Where the default rate comes from: the 3-month Treasury yield on FRED, refreshed daily by the same job that feeds the Macro Dashboard. It can be overwritten freely.
